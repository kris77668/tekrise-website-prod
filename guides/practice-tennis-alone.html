How to practise tennis alone

Last updated: 7 September 2026 By: the TEKRISE founder
THE SHORT ANSWER

You can improve without a coach, but only if something corrects your picture of your own swing — practising hard against a wrong mental model makes the error more automatic. Film yourself, work on one specific fault per session, and compare footage from the same camera position across weeks.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
  • The problem is not effort, it is feedback. Volume without correction makes errors automatic.
  • One fault, one cue, one session. Three corrections at once means none of them stick.
  • Serves with a basket are the best solo session — the shot you fully control and most players neglect.
  • Film the first five swings, before you warm up. That is the swing you actually play.
  • Most stroke faults are footwork faults in disguise. Movement work often beats stroke work.

01 The specific problem with practising alone

It is not motivation, and it is not effort. It is that you cannot see yourself. Every player has a mental model of what their swing looks like, and for almost everyone that model is wrong in at least one important way. Practising hard against a wrong model does not fix the model — it makes the error more automatic.

This is why a player can hit thousands of balls in a year and improve very little, while another player improves quickly with an hour a week of feedback. The variable is not volume. It is whether anything corrects the model.

02 A practice session that actually works

Unstructured hitting is pleasant and does very little. A session with a single defined objective beats three sessions of general hitting.

  • Pick one thing. One fault, one cue, one session. Not "work on my forehand" — "racquet back before the bounce".
  • Film the first five swings, before you warm into it. That is your baseline, and it is the swing you actually play in a match.
  • Work on the one thing for 20–30 minutes. Exaggerate the correction — a change that feels excessive usually looks about right on video.
  • Film again at the end. Same angle, same distance. This is the only way to know whether anything changed.
  • Finish by playing points or rallying freely, so the change has to survive contact with reality.

03 What to do with no partner and no court time

Shadow swings

Underrated because they look like nothing. Without a ball you can hold positions, exaggerate a correction and repeat it fifty times in five minutes. Film them — a shadow swing is much easier to analyse than a live one, and the movement pattern is what you are changing.

Wall practice

The ball comes back faster than off a court, which forces early preparation. That makes a wall an excellent tool for exactly one fault: preparing late. It is a poor tool for contact point, because the rushed rhythm distorts it.

Serve baskets

The only stroke you can genuinely practise alone at full quality. A basket of balls and an empty court is the highest-value solo session in tennis, because the serve is the shot you fully control and the one most club players neglect.

Footwork ladders and split-step drills

Since late arrival is the root cause of most technique faults, movement work without a racquet often improves your strokes more than stroke work does.

04 Tracking progress without a coach

Without someone watching, you need an external record or you will simply believe whatever you felt on the day — and feel is a famously unreliable narrator.

  • Film from the same position every session. Comparisons are meaningless if the camera moved.
  • Keep the clips. Month-to-month comparison shows changes that are invisible week to week.
  • Track one measurement, not a general impression. "Shoulder turn went from 28° to 41°" is progress. "Feeling better about my forehand" is weather.
  • Expect it to get worse before it gets better. A real technical change reliably makes you play worse for a week or two. Players who quit at that point conclude the change did not work, when in fact it had not finished.

05 Five mistakes solo players make

  • Working on everything at once. Three corrections means none of them become automatic.
  • Only filming good swings. You will analyse a shot you do not actually play.
  • Copying a professional’s swing. Copy the principle — early preparation, coil, contact in front. Do not copy the personal quirks; those are built on years of conditioning.
  • Practising only strokes. Most stroke faults are footwork faults wearing a disguise.
  • Confusing effort with improvement. Two focused hours beat six unfocused ones, every time.

06 Where a swing-analysis app fits

It closes exactly one gap: it tells you what your swing actually did, so you are working on the real fault instead of the one you assume you have. That is the piece a solo player is missing, and it is a genuinely large piece.

It does not replace a coach, a hitting partner, or court time. If you can afford occasional lessons, take them — and use the days in between to practise the thing your coach actually asked for, confirmed on video rather than by feel.

Frequently asked

Can you get better at tennis without a coach?
Yes, but only if something corrects your mental model of your own swing. Practising hard against a wrong model makes the error more automatic rather than fixing it. Film yourself, work on one specific fault at a time, and compare footage across sessions — that substitutes for the correction a coach would provide.
What should I practise alone if I have no hitting partner?
Serves with a basket of balls are the highest-value solo session, because the serve is the shot you fully control. After that: shadow swings for changing a movement pattern, wall practice specifically for late preparation, and footwork drills — since late arrival is the root cause of most technique faults.
How do I know if I am actually improving?
Film from the same position every session and track one specific measurement rather than a general feeling. “Shoulder turn went from 28° to 41°” is progress; “my forehand felt good today” is not. Month-to-month comparison reveals changes that are invisible week to week.
Is hitting against a wall good practice?
For one thing specifically: preparing earlier. The ball returns faster than off a court, which forces you to turn sooner. It is a poor tool for working on your contact point, because the rushed rhythm distorts where you meet the ball.
Why does my technique get worse when I change something?
Because it is supposed to. A real technical change reliably makes you play worse for a week or two while the new pattern is still conscious. Most players quit at exactly that point and conclude the change did not work, when in fact it had not finished.
