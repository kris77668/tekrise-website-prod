How to improve your tennis forehand

Last updated: 7 September 2026 By: the TEKRISE founder
THE SHORT ANSWER

Improve a forehand by fixing one specific thing at a time, in this order: arrive on balance, prepare earlier, turn your shoulders past your hips, meet the ball out in front with a long arm, and finish the swing. Working on several at once is the most common reason nothing changes.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
  • Prepare earlier is the cheapest gain — it needs no technical change, only earlier timing. Racquet back before the bounce.
  • Shoulder turn is where power comes from. Healthy peak hip–shoulder separation is 45–55°; most club players are under 35°.
  • Contact out in front, arm at 155–175°. A bent arm means the ball got too close — usually a footwork problem.
  • Fix footwork first. Late arrival causes the narrow stance, the rush and the lean-back all at once.
  • Expect to play worse for a week or two after a real change. That is the change working, not failing.

01 Why forehands plateau

Almost nobody plateaus from lack of effort. They plateau because the thing that is actually limiting the shot is invisible from inside it. You cannot see your own shoulder turn, you cannot feel that your coil peaked 40 ms after contact, and you certainly cannot see that your elbow was at 132° when it needed to be near 160°.

So players work on what they can perceive — usually "hit it harder" or "topspin more" — and grind away at a symptom while the cause sits untouched. The fastest way off a plateau is not more effort. It is finding out which one thing is limiting the shot, and working only on that until it changes.

02 The five things that actually change a forehand

In rough order of how much they matter for a club-level player, and how quickly they respond to work:

1. Prepare earlier — the free one

The single cheapest improvement available to most players, because it requires no technical change at all. You already own your swing; it just needs to start sooner. If your shoulder turn completes at the same moment you strike the ball, every shot is rushed and your technique never gets a chance to express itself.

The cue: racquet back before the bounce. Start the turn as the ball leaves your opponent’s strings, not as it crosses the net. A healthy swing has the coil peaking at least 120 ms before contact.

2. Turn your shoulders, not your arm

This is where effortless power comes from. Your shoulders should turn past your hips during the wind-up, storing elastic energy in your torso that releases into the ball. The measurement is hip–shoulder separation, and a healthy peak is 45–55°. Below 35° and the shot is essentially all arm, no matter how hard you swing.

The cue: at the end of your take-back, you should be able to glimpse your back shoulder in your peripheral vision. Turn your chest away from the net, not just your racquet away from your body.

3. Meet the ball out in front, with a long arm

A bent arm at contact means the ball is too close to you. You lose power, you lose the contact point, and you end up steering the ball rather than driving it. Healthy arm extension at contact is 155–175° — long, but not locked straight.

The cue: shadow-swing and freeze at contact. If you cannot straighten your hitting arm from that position without shuffling your feet, the ball is getting too close — which is usually a footwork problem, not an arm problem.

4. Get your weight moving forward

If your torso lean barely changes across the swing, your weight never travelled into the shot. Worse is falling backwards at contact, which leaks power and is a classic cause of balls flying long. A healthy contact has the torso leaning 5–15° forward.

The cue: feel the back heel lift as you swing. If you are consistently falling away, you are probably being crowded by the ball — take it earlier and move into it.

5. Finish the swing

A swing that stops near the ball was already decelerating through the ball, which costs both pace and spin. A full follow-through is not decoration; it is the evidence that you were still accelerating at contact. The racquet path after contact should be at least half the length of the path into it.

The cue: pick a finish position — over the opposite shoulder is the classic — and swing to it every single time.

03 Fix them in this order

These faults are not independent, and the order matters more than most players expect. Arriving late to the ball causes a narrow stance, rushed preparation and leaning back. Work on the arm while the feet are still wrong and you will fight the same problem for months.

04 How to film your own forehand usefully

Self-filming is the cheapest coaching upgrade available, and most people do it in a way that throws away the useful information.

  • Film from the side. Side-on gives the most usable data on a forehand — contact point, extension and weight transfer are all visible. Face-on is the best angle for judging stance width.
  • Get your whole body in frame, including your feet. Cropping the feet removes the footwork evidence, which is where the root cause usually is.
  • 15–60 seconds is plenty. You want a handful of representative swings, not a highlight reel.
  • Film your normal swing, not your best one. Trying hard for the camera measures a shot you do not actually play.
  • Same angle every time. Comparing across sessions only works if the camera did not move.

05 A self-check you can run today

Watch your own footage in slow motion and answer these, in this order. Stop at the first "no" — that is your project.

  • Are your feet still moving in the second before you hit, or are you planted and reaching?
  • Is the racquet back before the ball bounces?
  • At the end of your turn, has your chest turned away from the net — or only your arm?
  • At contact, is your hitting arm long and the ball clearly in front of your body?
  • Is your weight moving forward at contact, or are you falling backwards?
  • Does the racquet finish somewhere deliberate, or stop just past the ball?

Every one of these has a measurement behind it, listed with its healthy range in the swing fault glossary.

Frequently asked

Why has my forehand stopped improving?
Almost always because the limiting factor is something you cannot perceive from inside the shot — late preparation, a shoulder turn that never happens, or a contact point too close to your body. Players work on what they can feel, usually swinging harder, while the actual cause goes untouched. Filming the shot and checking one specific measurement at a time is what breaks the plateau.
What is the fastest way to add power to a forehand?
Shoulder turn. Coiling your shoulders past your hips stores elastic energy that releases into the ball, and it costs no extra effort. A healthy peak hip–shoulder separation is 45–55°; most club players sit below 35°, which makes the shot all arm no matter how hard they swing.
Where should I make contact on a forehand?
Out in front of your body, with the hitting arm extended to roughly 155–175° — long, but not locked straight at 180°. If you cannot straighten your arm at contact without shuffling your feet, the ball is getting too close, which is usually a footwork problem rather than an arm problem.
How should I film my forehand for analysis?
Side-on, with your whole body including your feet in frame, 15–60 seconds of your normal swing rather than your best one, and from the same camera position each session so you can compare. Cropping out the feet removes the footwork evidence, which is where the root cause usually lies.
How long does it take to fix a forehand?
One specific fault at a time, and expect a few weeks each. Preparation and footwork often improve within a fortnight because they need no technical change. Shoulder turn typically takes longer because it is a movement pattern rather than a timing adjustment. Working on several at once is the most common reason nothing changes.
